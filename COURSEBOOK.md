# 30-Day Conversational English Coursebook

A self-paced month of daily spoken English practice. Each day is designed for about 25–35 minutes: 3 minutes to recall, 10 minutes to learn from three conversations and phrase alternatives, 5 minutes for questions, 10–15 minutes for speaking, and 2 minutes to reflect. Study with a partner or speak both parts aloud. On review days, allow 10 extra minutes.

## Daily routine

1. **Recall:** Say yesterday’s useful phrases without looking. On Day 1, simply introduce yourself.
2. **Learn:** Read the theme, focus, teaching note, phrase families, and three model conversations. Repeat each exchange aloud twice, changing one detail to fit your life.
3. **Check:** Answer the four multiple-choice questions before reading the answer key. Say the correct answer in a full sentence.
4. **Speak and reflect:** Do the role-play, answer at least two open prompts, and record one phrase to use again tomorrow.

You can repeat any day. There is no requirement to complete lessons on consecutive calendar days. The website keeps an optional start date and saves progress in this browser.

## Day 1: Introductions

**Theme:** Names, hometowns, and first impressions.
**Goal:** Introduce yourself and keep a first meeting going
**Language focus:** Open questions and short answers

### Learn

Use “What do you do?” for work or study. “Where are you from?” asks about origin; “Where do you live?” asks about your current home. After answering, return the question.

**Useful phrases**

- "Hi, I'm ..."
- "Where are you from?"
- "It's nice to meet you."
- "What do you do?"

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Opening a chat**

- Hi, I’m …
- Hello, my name’s …
- I don’t think we’ve met—I’m …

**Asking about someone**

- Where are you based?
- What brings you here?
- How do you know the host?

**Keeping it going**

- How about you?
- What’s that like?
- How did you get started?

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: Hi, I’m Maya. Is this your first time here?
B: Yes, it is. I’m Sam. Nice to meet you.
A: Nice to meet you too. Where are you from?
B: I’m from Recife, but I live here now. How about you?

**Model conversation 2: At a workshop**

A: Hi, I’m Lina. Is this seat taken?
B: No, go ahead. I’m Theo.
A: Thanks. Have you been to one of these workshops before?
B: Once. The last one was really useful.

**Model conversation 3: At a neighbor’s gathering**

A: I don’t think we’ve met. I’m Noor, from upstairs.
B: I’m Alex. Good to meet you.
A: Likewise. How long have you lived here?
B: About six months. I’m still learning the area.

**Conversation note:** A friendly follow-up (“How about you?”) prevents the conversation from ending after one answer.

### Practice questions

1. Which question asks about a person's current home?
   - A. Where are you from?
   - B. What's your name?
   - C. Where do you live?

2. Choose the natural reply to “Nice to meet you.”
   - A. From my city.
   - B. Nice to meet you too.
   - C. I am meet.

3. What is a natural question at a workshop?
   - A. Have you been to one of these before?
   - B. Where is your passport?
   - C. Why are you sitting here?

4. Which response adds detail that invites another question?
   - A. Fine.
   - B. Yes.
   - C. I work at a small bookstore downtown.

5. Open response: How would you introduce yourself at a community event? Ask two different follow-up questions.

6. What might you say if you forget a person's name? Ask politely.

7. How could you introduce two people who have not met?

8. Role-play: You meet a new classmate. Exchange names, hometowns, and what you do; each person asks a follow-up.

**Answer key**

1. C. Where do you live? — “Live” points to the current place.
2. B. Nice to meet you too. — Return the greeting with “too.”
3. A. Have you been to one of these before? — It connects to the shared situation.
4. C. I work at a small bookstore downtown. — A specific detail gives your partner something to ask about.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 2: Family and friends

**Theme:** Relationships and describing people.
**Goal:** Describe relationships clearly and respectfully
**Language focus:** Present simple for facts; adjectives with examples

### Learn

“She’s thoughtful” is stronger when followed by evidence: “She checks in when I’m having a hard week.” “We get along” means you have a good relationship.

**Useful phrases**

- "I have ... siblings."
- "We're very close."
- "What's your family like?"
- "We've known each other for years."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Introducing someone**

- This is my cousin …
- I’d like you to meet my friend …
- We met through …

**Describing closeness**

- We’re close.
- We keep in touch.
- We’ve known each other since school.

**Describing character**

- She’s easy to talk to.
- He’s very dependable.
- They make people feel welcome.

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: Do you have any siblings?
B: Yes, an older brother. He lives nearby.
A: Are you two close?
B: We are. We talk most weekends, although we have different interests.

**Model conversation 2: Introducing a friend**

A: This is Priya, my friend from college.
B: Nice to meet you, Priya. How do you two know each other?
A: We studied together and still meet every month.
B: That’s lovely. What do you usually do together?

**Model conversation 3: Talking about a relative**

A: What’s your grandfather like?
B: He’s patient and funny. He always teaches me something new.
A: What has he taught you recently?
B: A few recipes from his childhood.

**Conversation note:** Ask about family with care: some people prefer to discuss friends or chosen family instead.

### Practice questions

2. Which sentence adds a supporting example?

3. Which phrase says a relationship continues despite distance?
   - A. We touch again.
   - B. We keep in touch.
   - C. We keep away.

4. Which question invites a concrete story?
   - A. What did you do together last time?
   - B. Are they nice?
   - C. Is your family family?

5. Open response: Who is important to you? Describe their personality and one example.

6. How do you stay connected with a friend who lives far away?

7. Who helped you through a difficult time, and how?

8. Role-play: Introduce someone important to you to a new friend; describe how you met and what you enjoy together.

**Answer key**

1. B. Get along — “Get along” describes a positive relationship.
2. A. My friend is kind because she helps new students. — Specific actions make descriptions clearer.
3. B. We keep in touch. — “Keep in touch” means stay in contact.
4. A. What did you do together last time? — It asks about an event, not just a label.

## Day 3: Daily routines

**Theme:** Habits, schedules, and frequency.
**Goal:** Talk about habits and compare days
**Language focus:** Frequency adverbs and time expressions

### Learn

Place “usually,” “often,” and “sometimes” before the main verb (“I usually cook”) but after “be” (“I’m usually home”). Use “on weekdays” for a repeating pattern.

**Useful phrases**

- "I usually ..."
- "I tend to ..."
- "What time do you normally ...?"
- "It depends on the day."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Starting a routine**

- My day starts with …
- First thing in the morning, I …
- I usually begin by …

**Talking about frequency**

- Most days, I …
- Every now and then, I …
- I hardly ever …

**Explaining a change**

- On weekends, I … instead.
- When I’m busy, I …
- It depends on my schedule.

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: What does a typical weekday look like for you?
B: I usually start work at nine, then take a break around noon.
A: Do you do the same thing on weekends?
B: Not really. I tend to sleep later and meet friends.

**Model conversation 2: Before work**

A: Are you an early bird?
B: Usually. I get up at six and read for ten minutes.
A: Do you always have time for that?
B: Not when I have an early meeting. Then I read at night.

**Model conversation 3: Comparing weekends**

A: What do your Sundays look like?
B: I normally cook a big lunch and call my parents.
A: That sounds relaxing. Is Saturday similar?
B: Not at all. Saturday is usually full of errands.

**Conversation note:** “It depends on the day” is a useful honest answer when your schedule changes.

### Practice questions

1. Which is natural?
   - A. I usually eat breakfast at eight.
   - B. I eat usually breakfast at eight.
   - C. Usually I am eat breakfast.

2. Choose the natural phrase for a repeated weekday habit.
   - A. At weekday I take bus.
   - B. In weekdays bus take.
   - C. On weekdays I take the bus.

3. What does “hardly ever” mean?
   - A. Quite often
   - B. Almost never
   - C. Every day

4. Which follow-up explores a change in routine?
   - A. What do you do when you're busy?
   - B. Is that a day?
   - C. Are you time?

5. Open response: Walk through your morning in order. What changes on weekends?

6. Which part of your routine would you like to change?

7. Tell someone about a routine that helps you feel prepared.

8. Role-play: Ask a partner about their weekday and weekend routines; find two similarities and one difference.

**Answer key**

1. A. I usually eat breakfast at eight. — Frequency adverbs normally precede the main verb.
2. C. On weekdays I take the bus. — “On weekdays” names the recurring days.
3. B. Almost never — It describes a very rare habit.
4. A. What do you do when you're busy? — It asks how circumstances affect the habit.

## Day 4: Hobbies and free time

**Theme:** Interests and invitations.
**Goal:** Share interests and invite participation
**Language focus:** “Be into” and invitations with “Would you like”

### Learn

“I’m into photography” sounds conversational. Ask “How did you get into it?” to invite a story. For a polite invitation, use “Would you like to join me?”

**Useful phrases**

- "I'm into ..."
- "How did you get into that?"
- "I do it whenever I can."
- "Would you like to join me?"

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Stating an interest**

- I’m really into …
- I enjoy … in my free time.
- I’ve recently taken up …

**Asking about an interest**

- What do you like about it?
- How often do you do it?
- How did you get into it?

**Inviting someone**

- Want to give it a try?
- Would you be interested in joining?
- Come along if you’d like.

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: What do you do for fun?
B: I’m into hiking, especially short trails near town.
A: How did you get into that?
B: A friend invited me last year. Would you like to join us sometime?

**Model conversation 2: A new hobby**

A: I’ve recently taken up pottery.
B: That sounds fun. What do you like about it?
A: I like working with my hands. It helps me slow down.
B: Do you go to a class or practice at home?

**Model conversation 3: An invitation**

A: A few of us are playing badminton on Sunday. Want to come along?
B: I haven’t played in years. Is it okay if I’m a beginner?
A: Absolutely. We’re just playing for fun.
B: In that case, I’d love to.

**Conversation note:** Give a brief description of what the activity involves, especially if your listener is new to it.

### Practice questions

1. What does “I’m into painting” mean?
   - A. I am inside paint.
   - B. I dislike painting.
   - C. I enjoy painting.

2. Which is a polite invitation?
   - A. Why not joined us?
   - B. Would you like to join us?
   - C. You join us now.

3. Which phrase suggests a new hobby?
   - A. I took in pottery later.
   - B. I've recently taken up pottery.
   - C. I have pottery taken down.

4. Which reply makes a beginner feel welcome?
   - A. You must be excellent.
   - B. Beginners cannot come.
   - C. We're just playing for fun.

5. Open response: What hobby have you kept or recently tried? Why?

6. What hobby would you try if equipment and cost were no issue?

7. How would you decline an invitation kindly and suggest another time?

8. Role-play: Explain your hobby to a beginner, answer their questions, and invite them to a suitable first session.

**Answer key**

1. C. I enjoy painting. — “Be into” means to be interested in something.
2. B. Would you like to join us? — “Would you like” offers a choice politely.
3. B. I've recently taken up pottery. — “Take up” means begin an activity.
4. C. We're just playing for fun. — It removes pressure.

## Day 5: Food preferences

**Theme:** Likes, dislikes, and recommendations.
**Goal:** Express food preferences and make recommendations
**Language focus:** Preferences with “prefer” and “would rather”

### Learn

Use “I prefer tea to coffee” for a general preference and “I’d rather have tea” for a choice now. State allergies or dietary needs clearly rather than as a casual dislike.

**Useful phrases**

- "I'm a big fan of ..."
- "I can't eat ..."
- "What would you recommend?"
- "I've never tried that before."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Expressing likes**

- I love the flavor of …
- I’m partial to …
- I could eat … every week.

**Stating a preference**

- I’d go for …
- I prefer … to …
- I’m in the mood for …

**Checking needs**

- Does it contain …?
- Is there a vegetarian option?
- How spicy is it?

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: What should I try here?
B: The vegetable noodles are excellent. Do you like spicy food?
A: A little, but I can’t eat peanuts.
B: Let’s check the ingredients before ordering.

**Model conversation 2: Choosing lunch**

A: What are you in the mood for?
B: Something light. Maybe soup and bread.
A: The tomato soup is good, but it has cream.
B: Thanks for telling me. I’ll choose the lentil soup instead.

**Model conversation 3: Recommending a dish**

A: What’s a good local dish to try?
B: The rice cakes are popular. They’re savory and a little crisp.
A: Are they spicy?
B: Not usually, but ask about the sauce.

**Conversation note:** When recommending food, ask about restrictions first and describe taste, texture, or spice level.

### Practice questions

3. Which question checks an ingredient?

   - B. Does it contain sesame?
   - C. Is it sesame feeling?

4. Which phrase describes a choice right now?
   - A. I mood in soup.
   - B. I'm in the mood for soup.
   - C. I have mood soup every day.

5. Open response: Describe a favorite meal with two taste words and one reason you enjoy it.

6. What food reminds you of home, and why?

7. Describe a dish so someone unfamiliar with it can imagine the taste.

8. Role-play: Recommend a dish to a visitor, check for dietary needs, and suggest an alternative.

**Answer key**

1. B. I prefer tea to coffee. — Use “prefer X to Y.”
2. A. I can't eat peanuts. — Direct wording avoids misunderstandings.
3. B. Does it contain sesame? — “Contain” asks what is in a dish.
4. B. I'm in the mood for soup. — “In the mood for” expresses a current desire.

## Day 6: Ordering at a cafe

**Theme:** Polite requests and clarifying an order.
**Goal:** Order politely and confirm details
**Language focus:** Could I / Can I for requests; without and with

### Learn

“Could I get a small coffee?” is polite. “Without milk” removes an ingredient; “with oat milk” specifies a replacement. Repeat the final order if anything is unclear.

**Useful phrases**

- "Could I get ...?"
- "For here or to go?"
- "Can I have that without ...?"
- "That's all, thank you."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Making an order**

- I’ll have …, please.
- May I have …?
- Could I order …?

**Changing an item**

- Could you leave out …?
- Could I swap … for …?
- Can I have it on the side?

**Checking the bill**

- How much is that altogether?
- Can I pay by card?
- Could I have the receipt?

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: Hi, could I get an iced latte with oat milk?
B: Of course. What size would you like?
A: Small, please. And a cheese sandwich for here.
B: One small iced latte with oat milk and one sandwich. Anything else?

**Model conversation 2: A busy morning**

A: Morning. Could I order a cappuccino and a bagel?
B: Sure. Would you like the bagel toasted?
A: Yes, please, with the cream cheese on the side.
B: Got it. That’ll be seven dollars.

**Model conversation 3: Correcting an order**

A: Sorry, I asked for the iced version.
B: My apologies. I’ll make a new one right away.
A: Thanks. No rush.
B: I appreciate your patience.

**Conversation note:** “For here” means you will eat or drink at the cafe; “to go” means you will take it away.

6. How would you order if an item on the menu is unavailable?

8. Role-play: You are the customer; a partner is the server. The server asks about size and repeats the order for confirmation.

**Answer key**

1. A. Could I get a small tea, please? — “Could I” and “please” soften the request.
2. C. Take it away. — “To go” describes takeaway service.
3. B. Served separately — It asks for a separate serving.
4. A. Sorry, I asked for the iced version. — State the requested item calmly.

## Day 7: Weekly review and small talk

**Theme:** Starting and keeping a light conversation going.
**Goal:** Build a short conversation from familiar topics
**Language focus:** Follow-up questions and natural transitions

### Learn

Avoid a chain of unrelated questions. Respond to the other person first, then ask a question connected to their answer: “You mentioned hiking. Where do you usually go?”

**Useful phrases**

- "How's your week going?"
- "That sounds interesting."
- "Tell me more about that."
- "Speaking of ..."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Showing interest**

- Really? Tell me more.
- That sounds great.
- What was that like?

**Connecting ideas**

- Speaking of …
- That reminds me of …
- You mentioned … earlier.

**Taking turns**

- How about you?
- Have you had a similar experience?
- What do you think?

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: How’s your week going?
B: Pretty well. I tried a new cafe yesterday.
A: That sounds nice. What did you order?
B: A chai latte. They also had excellent pastries.
A: Speaking of food, have you tried the bakery near the station?

**Model conversation 2: Following up**

A: I finally started drawing again.
B: That’s great. What made you start?
A: My sister gave me a sketchbook.
B: Nice. What was the first thing you drew?

**Model conversation 3: Changing direction**

A: I’ve been cooking more at home.
B: That reminds me—I found a weekend food market.
A: Where is it?
B: Near the library. Would you like to go sometime?

**Conversation note:** A natural conversation shares turns. Answer questions with a little detail so your partner has something to ask about.

### Practice questions

1. Which response encourages more conversation?
   - A. Okay.
   - B. Next subject.
   - C. That sounds interesting. How did it go?

2. Choose a connected follow-up to “I started running.”

   - B. Where do you usually run?
   - C. What color is your phone?

3. Which question follows “I started drawing”?
   - A. Do you own shoes?
   - B. What do you like to draw?
   - C. What time is the train?

4. Which expression links a new idea to an old one?

5. Open response: Which topic from Days 1–6 was easiest? Which needs more practice?

6. Which follow-up question made someone open up recently?

7. Tell a short story, then invite the other person to share one.

8. Role-play: Hold a five-minute conversation that includes at least three earlier topics and four follow-up questions.

**Answer key**

1. C. That sounds interesting. How did it go? — It acknowledges and asks a connected question.
2. B. Where do you usually run? — Ask about the activity just mentioned.
3. B. What do you like to draw? — It continues the topic.
4. C. That reminds me of … — It introduces an association.

## Day 8: Weather and seasons

**Theme:** Weather, clothing, and plans.
**Goal:** Describe weather and adjust plans
**Language focus:** It is + adjective; conditional “if”

### Learn

Say “It’s sunny,” “It’s raining,” or “It’s been chilly lately.” For a possible future event, use “If it rains, we can go inside,” not “If it will rain.”

**Useful phrases**

- "It's been really ... lately."
- "What's the forecast?"
- "I prefer ... weather."
- "If it rains, we can ..."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Describing today**

- It's bright and breezy.
- It looks like rain.
- It's getting warmer.

**Sharing a preference**

- I don't mind the cold.
- I love this kind of weather.
- I'm not keen on humidity.

**Adjusting plans**

- Let's wait and see.
- We can move it indoors.
- Shall we make a backup plan?

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: What’s the forecast for Saturday?
B: It looks cloudy, with a chance of rain.
A: Shall we still go to the park?
B: Let’s decide in the morning. If it rains, we can visit the museum.

**Model conversation 2: Planning a picnic**

A: It looks like rain this afternoon.
B: Should we postpone the picnic?
A: Maybe. Let’s check again at noon.
B: Good idea. If it rains, we could eat at my place.

**Model conversation 3: Different seasons**

A: Do you like winter here?
B: I do, especially when it’s clear and cold.
A: I prefer spring. The days are longer.
B: Fair enough. What do you usually do outdoors then?

**Conversation note:** Weather is useful small talk, but connect it to a preference or plan to make the exchange richer.

6. How does the weather change your energy or mood?

7. Describe a favorite season without using the word “nice.”

8. Role-play: Plan an outdoor outing with a backup indoor option.

**Answer key**

2. A. It's been windy lately. — “It’s been” connects past days to now.

## Day 9: Neighborhood and city

**Theme:** Places, atmosphere, and local tips.
**Goal:** Describe your neighborhood to a newcomer
**Language focus:** There is/are; distance expressions

### Learn

“There’s a library nearby” introduces a place. “It’s within walking distance” means you can reasonably walk there. Describe both atmosphere and practical features.

**Useful phrases**

- "I live near ..."
- "It's within walking distance."
- "There's a great ... nearby."
- "What's your neighborhood like?"

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Describing atmosphere**

- It's lively in the evenings.
- It has a relaxed feel.
- It's fairly quiet at night.

**Locating a place**

- It's around the corner.
- It's a short walk away.
- It's just past the station.

**Recommending a spot**

- You might like …
- I’d suggest …
- It's worth visiting for …

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: What’s your neighborhood like?
B: It’s lively but fairly quiet at night. There’s a park and a small market nearby.
A: Is it easy to get around?
B: Yes. The train station is within walking distance.

**Model conversation 2: A newcomer asks**

A: Is there somewhere to study nearby?
B: There’s a library around the corner. It’s quiet upstairs.
A: Does it open on weekends?
B: I think so, but let’s check the hours online.

**Model conversation 3: A neighborhood walk**

A: What’s that little shop across the street?
B: It’s a bakery. They make fresh bread in the mornings.
A: Should we stop in?
B: Definitely. The line is shorter before ten.

**Conversation note:** Local recommendations are more useful when you mention who the place is good for and when to go.

## Day 29: Current events and community

**Theme:** Discussing a local event without assuming agreement.
**Goal:** Discuss a local event with curiosity
**Language focus:** Source, fact, uncertainty, and opinion

### Learn

“I heard about the new community garden” states what you encountered. “From what I understand…” signals a tentative summary. Distinguish what is confirmed from what you think about it.

**Useful phrases**

- "I heard about ..."
- "From what I understand ..."
- "How do you feel about it?"
- "I haven't looked into it enough yet."

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Referring to a source**

- According to the library notice …
- I saw an announcement from …
- The organizer says …

**Showing uncertainty**

- I think it starts next week.
- I'm not sure of the exact date.
- I'd need to check.

**Inviting another view**

- What do you make of it?
- Would that interest you?
- Have you heard more about it?

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

A: I heard the library is hosting evening language exchanges.
B: That sounds useful. Do you know when they start?

B: Let's check the library's announcement before making plans.

**Model conversation 2: A community class**

## Day 30: Final conversation and reflection

**Theme:** Combining stories, opinions, questions, and plans.
**Goal:** Combine skills and choose your next practice cycle
**Language focus:** Review, self-correction, and next steps

### Learn

Use familiar phrases flexibly rather than memorizing a perfect speech. In a long conversation, vary questions, give reasons, tell one story, and repair any misunderstanding.

**Useful phrases**

- "One thing I've learned is ..."
- "I feel more confident about ..."
- "I'd like to keep improving ..."
- "What should we talk about next?"

**Ways to say it: choose a phrase that fits your situation**

**Reflecting on progress**

- I've become more comfortable with …
- I'm proud that I can …
- I still want to work on …

**Keeping a chat going**

- Tell me more about that.
- What happened next?
- How about you?

**Planning ahead**

- Next month, I'll focus on …
- I'll keep a list of …
- I'll practice by …

**Model conversation 1: Everyday example**

**Conversation note:** Judge progress by what you can communicate, not only by grammatical accuracy. Note one strength and one next step.
