# 30-Day Conversational English Coursebook

A self-paced month of daily spoken English practice. Each day is designed for about 20–25 minutes: 3 minutes to review, 5 minutes to read and repeat, 5 minutes for questions, 5–10 minutes for a role-play, and 2 minutes to reflect. Study with a partner or speak both parts aloud. On review days, allow 10 extra minutes.

## Daily routine

1. **Recall:** Say yesterday’s useful phrases without looking. On Day 1, simply introduce yourself.
2. **Learn:** Read the theme, focus, teaching note, phrases, and model dialogue. Repeat each line aloud twice, changing one detail to fit your life.
3. **Check:** Answer the two multiple-choice questions before reading the answer key. Say the correct answer in a full sentence.
4. **Speak and reflect:** Do the role-play, answer the open prompt for at least two minutes, and record one phrase to use again tomorrow.

You can repeat any day. There is no requirement to complete lessons on consecutive calendar days. The website keeps an optional start date and saves progress in this browser.

## Day 1: Introductions

**Theme:** Names, hometowns, and first impressions.
**Goal:** Introduce yourself and keep a first meeting going
**Language focus:** Open questions and short answers

### Learn

Use “What do you do?” for work or study. “Where are you from?” asks about origin; “Where do you live?” asks about your current home. After answering, return the question.

**Useful phrases**

- "Hi, I'm ..."
- "Where are you from?"
- "It's nice to meet you."
- "What do you do?"

**Model conversation**

A: Hi, I’m Maya. Is this your first time here?
B: Yes, it is. I’m Sam. Nice to meet you.
A: Nice to meet you too. Where are you from?
B: I’m from Recife, but I live here now. How about you?

**Conversation note:** A friendly follow-up (“How about you?”) prevents the conversation from ending after one answer.

### Practice questions

1. Which question asks about a person's current home?
   - A. Where are you from?
   - B. What's your name?
   - C. Where do you live?

2. Choose the natural reply to “Nice to meet you.”
   - A. From my city.
   - B. Nice to meet you too.
   - C. I am meet.

3. Open response: How would you introduce yourself at a community event? Ask two different follow-up questions.

4. Role-play: You meet a new classmate. Exchange names, hometowns, and what you do; each person asks a follow-up.

**Answer key**

1. C. Where do you live? — “Live” points to the current place.
2. B. Nice to meet you too. — Return the greeting with “too.”

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 2: Family and friends

**Theme:** Relationships and describing people.
**Goal:** Describe relationships clearly and respectfully
**Language focus:** Present simple for facts; adjectives with examples

### Learn

“She’s thoughtful” is stronger when followed by evidence: “She checks in when I’m having a hard week.” “We get along” means you have a good relationship.

**Useful phrases**

- "I have ... siblings."
- "We're very close."
- "What's your family like?"
- "We've known each other for years."

**Model conversation**

A: Do you have any siblings?
B: Yes, an older brother. He lives nearby.
A: Are you two close?
B: We are. We talk most weekends, although we have different interests.

**Conversation note:** Ask about family with care: some people prefer to discuss friends or chosen family instead.

### Practice questions

1. Which phrase means “have a good relationship”?
   - A. Get over
   - B. Get along
   - C. Get around

2. Which sentence adds a supporting example?
   - A. My friend is kind because she helps new students.
   - B. My friend is kind.
   - C. Friend kind helping.

3. Open response: Who is important to you? Describe their personality and one example.

4. Role-play: Introduce someone important to you to a new friend; describe how you met and what you enjoy together.

**Answer key**

1. B. Get along — “Get along” describes a positive relationship.
2. A. My friend is kind because she helps new students. — Specific actions make descriptions clearer.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 3: Daily routines

**Theme:** Habits, schedules, and frequency.
**Goal:** Talk about habits and compare days
**Language focus:** Frequency adverbs and time expressions

### Learn

Place “usually,” “often,” and “sometimes” before the main verb (“I usually cook”) but after “be” (“I’m usually home”). Use “on weekdays” for a repeating pattern.

**Useful phrases**

- "I usually ..."
- "I tend to ..."
- "What time do you normally ...?"
- "It depends on the day."

**Model conversation**

A: What does a typical weekday look like for you?
B: I usually start work at nine, then take a break around noon.
A: Do you do the same thing on weekends?
B: Not really. I tend to sleep later and meet friends.

**Conversation note:** “It depends on the day” is a useful honest answer when your schedule changes.

### Practice questions

1. Which is natural?
   - A. I usually eat breakfast at eight.
   - B. I eat usually breakfast at eight.
   - C. Usually I am eat breakfast.

2. Choose the natural phrase for a repeated weekday habit.
   - A. At weekday I take bus.
   - B. In weekdays bus take.
   - C. On weekdays I take the bus.

3. Open response: Walk through your morning in order. What changes on weekends?

4. Role-play: Ask a partner about their weekday and weekend routines; find two similarities and one difference.

**Answer key**

1. A. I usually eat breakfast at eight. — Frequency adverbs normally precede the main verb.
2. C. On weekdays I take the bus. — “On weekdays” names the recurring days.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 4: Hobbies and free time

**Theme:** Interests and invitations.
**Goal:** Share interests and invite participation
**Language focus:** “Be into” and invitations with “Would you like”

### Learn

“I’m into photography” sounds conversational. Ask “How did you get into it?” to invite a story. For a polite invitation, use “Would you like to join me?”

**Useful phrases**

- "I'm into ..."
- "How did you get into that?"
- "I do it whenever I can."
- "Would you like to join me?"

**Model conversation**

A: What do you do for fun?
B: I’m into hiking, especially short trails near town.
A: How did you get into that?
B: A friend invited me last year. Would you like to join us sometime?

**Conversation note:** Give a brief description of what the activity involves, especially if your listener is new to it.

### Practice questions

3. Open response: What hobby have you kept or recently tried? Why?

4. Role-play: Explain your hobby to a beginner, answer their questions, and invite them to a suitable first session.

**Answer key**

1. C. I enjoy painting. — “Be into” means to be interested in something.
2. B. Would you like to join us? — “Would you like” offers a choice politely.

## Day 5: Food preferences

**Theme:** Likes, dislikes, and recommendations.
**Goal:** Express food preferences and make recommendations
**Language focus:** Preferences with “prefer” and “would rather”

### Learn

Use “I prefer tea to coffee” for a general preference and “I’d rather have tea” for a choice now. State allergies or dietary needs clearly rather than as a casual dislike.

**Useful phrases**

- "I'm a big fan of ..."
- "I can't eat ..."
- "What would you recommend?"
- "I've never tried that before."

**Model conversation**

A: What should I try here?
B: The vegetable noodles are excellent. Do you like spicy food?
A: A little, but I can’t eat peanuts.
B: Let’s check the ingredients before ordering.

**Conversation note:** When recommending food, ask about restrictions first and describe taste, texture, or spice level.

### Practice questions

1. Which sentence describes a general preference?
   - A. I prefer tea than coffee.
   - B. I prefer tea to coffee.
   - C. I rather tea than coffee.

2. How should you clearly state a food restriction?
   - A. I can't eat peanuts.
   - B. Peanuts are not my favorite.
   - C. Maybe peanuts no.

3. Open response: Describe a favorite meal with two taste words and one reason you enjoy it.

4. Role-play: Recommend a dish to a visitor, check for dietary needs, and suggest an alternative.

**Answer key**

1. B. I prefer tea to coffee. — Use “prefer X to Y.”
2. A. I can't eat peanuts. — Direct wording avoids misunderstandings.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 6: Ordering at a cafe

**Theme:** Polite requests and clarifying an order.
**Goal:** Order politely and confirm details
**Language focus:** Could I / Can I for requests; without and with

### Learn

“Could I get a small coffee?” is polite. “Without milk” removes an ingredient; “with oat milk” specifies a replacement. Repeat the final order if anything is unclear.

**Useful phrases**

- "Could I get ...?"
- "For here or to go?"
- "Can I have that without ...?"
- "That's all, thank you."

**Model conversation**

A: Hi, could I get an iced latte with oat milk?
B: Of course. What size would you like?
A: Small, please. And a cheese sandwich for here.
B: One small iced latte with oat milk and one sandwich. Anything else?

**Conversation note:** “For here” means you will eat or drink at the cafe; “to go” means you will take it away.

### Practice questions

1. Which request is both clear and polite?
   - A. Could I get a small tea, please?
   - B. Give tea.
   - C. I need tea you.

2. What does “to go” mean?
   - A. Eat it inside.
   - B. Pay later.
   - C. Take it away.

3. Open response: Order two items, modify one, and specify for here or to go.

4. Role-play: You are the customer; a partner is the server. The server asks about size and repeats the order for confirmation.

**Answer key**

1. A. Could I get a small tea, please? — “Could I” and “please” soften the request.
2. C. Take it away. — “To go” describes takeaway service.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 7: Weekly review and small talk

**Theme:** Starting and keeping a light conversation going.
**Goal:** Build a short conversation from familiar topics
**Language focus:** Follow-up questions and natural transitions

### Learn

Avoid a chain of unrelated questions. Respond to the other person first, then ask a question connected to their answer: “You mentioned hiking. Where do you usually go?”

**Useful phrases**

- "How's your week going?"
- "That sounds interesting."
- "Tell me more about that."
- "Speaking of ..."

**Model conversation**

A: How’s your week going?
B: Pretty well. I tried a new cafe yesterday.
A: That sounds nice. What did you order?
B: A chai latte. They also had excellent pastries.
A: Speaking of food, have you tried the bakery near the station?

**Conversation note:** A natural conversation shares turns. Answer questions with a little detail so your partner has something to ask about.

### Practice questions

1. Which response encourages more conversation?
   - A. Okay.
   - B. Next subject.
   - C. That sounds interesting. How did it go?

2. Choose a connected follow-up to “I started running.”
   - A. Do you know the time?
   - B. Where do you usually run?
   - C. What color is your phone?

3. Open response: Which topic from Days 1–6 was easiest? Which needs more practice?

4. Role-play: Hold a five-minute conversation that includes at least three earlier topics and four follow-up questions.

**Answer key**

1. C. That sounds interesting. How did it go? — It acknowledges and asks a connected question.
2. B. Where do you usually run? — Ask about the activity just mentioned.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 8: Weather and seasons

**Theme:** Weather, clothing, and plans.
**Goal:** Describe weather and adjust plans
**Language focus:** It is + adjective; conditional “if”

### Learn

Say “It’s sunny,” “It’s raining,” or “It’s been chilly lately.” For a possible future event, use “If it rains, we can go inside,” not “If it will rain.”

**Useful phrases**

- "It's been really ... lately."
- "What's the forecast?"
- "I prefer ... weather."
- "If it rains, we can ..."

**Model conversation**

A: What’s the forecast for Saturday?
B: It looks cloudy, with a chance of rain.
A: Shall we still go to the park?
B: Let’s decide in the morning. If it rains, we can visit the museum.

**Conversation note:** Weather is useful small talk, but connect it to a preference or plan to make the exchange richer.

### Practice questions

1. Which sentence is natural?
   - A. If raining, go can.
   - B. If it rains, we can go inside.
   - C. If it will rain, we can inside.

2. Which phrase describes recent weather?
   - A. It's been windy lately.
   - B. It windy has lately.
   - C. Wind lately be.

3. Open response: Describe today's weather and how it affects your plans.

4. Role-play: Plan an outdoor outing with a backup indoor option.

**Answer key**

1. B. If it rains, we can go inside. — Use present tense after “if” for this future possibility.
2. A. It's been windy lately. — “It’s been” connects past days to now.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 9: Neighborhood and city

**Theme:** Places, atmosphere, and local tips.
**Goal:** Describe your neighborhood to a newcomer
**Language focus:** There is/are; distance expressions

### Learn

“There’s a library nearby” introduces a place. “It’s within walking distance” means you can reasonably walk there. Describe both atmosphere and practical features.

**Useful phrases**

- "I live near ..."
- "It's within walking distance."
- "There's a great ... nearby."
- "What's your neighborhood like?"

**Model conversation**

A: What’s your neighborhood like?
B: It’s lively but fairly quiet at night. There’s a park and a small market nearby.
A: Is it easy to get around?
B: Yes. The train station is within walking distance.

**Conversation note:** Local recommendations are more useful when you mention who the place is good for and when to go.

### Practice questions

1. Which phrase says a place is nearby on foot?

   - C. Under distance foot

3. Open response: What are two places a visitor should know in your area?

4. Role-play: Introduce your neighborhood to someone moving in; recommend a place to eat and a way to travel.

**Answer key**

1. A. Within walking distance — This is the conventional distance phrase.
2. C. There's a park nearby. — Use “there is” with a singular noun.

## Day 10: Directions and transportation

**Theme:** Getting around and checking understanding.
**Goal:** Give and check directions
**Language focus:** Imperatives and sequencing

### Learn

Use short steps: “Go straight,” “Turn left at the lights,” “It’s next to the bank.” Check understanding with “So I turn right after the bridge?”

**Useful phrases**

- "How do I get to ...?"
- "Go straight and turn left."
- "Is it far from here?"
- "So I take the next right?"

**Model conversation**

A: Excuse me, how do I get to the library?
B: Go straight for two blocks, then turn right at the traffic lights.
A: Is it on the left?
B: Yes, just past the pharmacy.

**Conversation note:** Landmarks such as stations and shops can be easier to follow than street names alone.

### Practice questions

1. What comes after “turn right at”?
   - A. To traffic lights
   - B. Traffic lights on
   - C. The traffic lights

2. Which sentence checks understanding?
   - A. Where you say again all?
   - B. So I take the next left?
   - C. You not right?

3. Open response: Give directions from your home to a nearby shop using three steps.

4. Role-play: A visitor asks for a station; give directions and have them repeat the route back.

**Answer key**

1. C. The traffic lights — “At” introduces the point where you turn.
2. B. So I take the next left? — Restating a step lets the speaker confirm it.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 11: Shopping

**Theme:** Sizes, prices, and decisions.
**Goal:** Compare and choose an item in a store
**Language focus:** Comparatives and polite requests

### Learn

Use “This one is cheaper than that one” and “It fits better.” Asking “Can I try it on?” is appropriate for clothing; “Can I see it?” works for other items.

**Useful phrases**

- "Do you have this in another size?"
- "How much is it?"
- "Can I try it on?"
- "I'll think about it."

A: Do you have this jacket in a medium?
B: Let me check. Here you are.
A: Thanks. Can I try it on?
B: Of course. The fitting room is over there.
A: This size fits better. How much is it?

**Conversation note:** “I’ll think about it” politely leaves a decision open without promising to buy.

**Answer key**

## Day 12: Making plans

**Theme:** Availability and agreeing on details.
**Goal:** Agree on a specific plan
**Language focus:** Invitations, alternatives, and confirmation

### Learn

A plan is complete when both people know the day, time, place, and activity. If a suggestion does not work, offer an alternative: “I can’t do Friday. How about Saturday?”

**Useful phrases**

- "Are you free on ...?"
- "How about ...?"
- "That works for me."
- "Let's meet at ..."

**Model conversation**

A: Are you free on Saturday afternoon?
B: I think so. What did you have in mind?
A: Coffee around three?
B: That works. Let’s meet outside the station at 2:45 and walk over.

**Conversation note:** “That works for me” accepts a suggestion. Repeat the final details to prevent confusion.

### Practice questions

1. Which reply proposes an alternative?
   - A. Friday doesn't work. How about Sunday?
   - B. No.
   - C. Sunday maybe is no.

2. What information makes a meeting plan complete?
   - A. Only the day

   - C. Day, time, place, and activity

3. Open response: Invite someone to an activity and offer a backup time.

4. Role-play: Plan a weekend outing; your partner is unavailable at your first suggested time.

**Answer key**

1. A. Friday doesn't work. How about Sunday? — A clear alternative keeps planning moving.
2. C. Day, time, place, and activity — Both people need the practical details.

## Day 14: Weekly review and storytelling

**Theme:** Sequencing a recent experience.
**Goal:** Tell a short story in order
**Language focus:** Past tense and sequencing markers

### Learn

Anchor the listener: when and where did it happen? Then use “At first,” “Then,” and “In the end.” End with why the event mattered, not only what happened.

**Useful phrases**

- "At first ..."
- "Then ..."
- "After that ..."
- "In the end ..."

**Model conversation**

A: Did anything interesting happen this week?
B: Yes. On Tuesday, I missed my usual bus. At first, I was frustrated.
A: What happened next?
B: I walked a different route, found a new bakery, and arrived on time after all.

**Conversation note:** Pause between events; listeners can ask “What happened next?” or “How did you feel?”

### Practice questions

1. Which phrase signals the conclusion?
   - A. Before that
   - B. In the end
   - C. At first

2. What should a clear story establish near the start?
   - A. When and where it happened
   - B. Every small detail
   - C. The final opinion only

3. Open response: Tell a story about a small surprise this week, including your reaction.

4. Role-play: Tell a two-minute story while a partner asks two follow-up questions.

**Answer key**

1. B. In the end — “In the end” introduces the outcome.
2. A. When and where it happened — Context helps the listener follow events.

**After speaking:** What did you express clearly? Which phrase will you reuse tomorrow?

## Day 17: Health and well-being

**Theme:** Everyday symptoms and self-care.
**Goal:** Describe a minor health issue and show care
**Language focus:** Symptoms with “have” and duration with “since/for”

### Learn

Say “I have a headache,” “I feel tired,” and “I’ve had a headache since morning.” “Since” names a starting point; “for” names a length of time.

**Useful phrases**

- "I'm feeling a bit ..."
- "I've had a headache since ..."
- "I need to rest."
- "How are you feeling now?"

**Model conversation**

A: You look tired. Are you okay?
B: I have a headache. I’ve felt unwell since this morning.
A: I’m sorry to hear that. Do you need a break?
B: Yes, I think I’ll rest for a while.

**Conversation note:** Avoid making assumptions about someone’s health. Ask what support they would find helpful.

### Practice questions

4. Role-play: A colleague feels unwell. Ask how they are, offer practical help, and respect their answer.

**Answer key**

1. B. Since this morning — “Since” goes with a starting time.

## Day 18: Exercise and fitness

**Theme:** Activities, goals, and routines.
**Goal:** Talk about movement and realistic goals
**Language focus:** Frequency, ability, and gradual change

### Learn

Use “I go for a walk three times a week” for frequency and “I’m trying to build up to 30 minutes” for a goal. “Let’s take it easy” suggests a comfortable pace.

**Useful phrases**

- "I try to stay active by ..."
- "How often do you ...?"
- "I'm trying to improve my ..."
- "Let's take it easy."

**Model conversation**

A: How do you stay active?
B: I walk after lunch when I can. I’m trying to be more consistent.
A: Would you like to go together tomorrow?
B: Sure, but let’s take it easy. I’m just getting started.

**Conversation note:** Different bodies and schedules need different routines; frame your own preferences without judging others.

### Practice questions

1. Which phrase describes frequency?
   - A. Three times a week
   - B. At three weeks
